# Module 1 lesson — Say which stocks you mean

Time: about 60 minutes, including the walkthrough, worksheet, and quiz.

## The question that opens the course

How many stocks are in the Indian stock market?

Guess before you read on. Fifty is a reasonable answer; the Nifty 50 fills
the evening news. Five hundred is reasonable too, since Marketworks talks
about 500 stocks constantly. Scroll a broker app for a minute and you might
say several thousand.

Each answer counts something real. None of them is "the market."

An election result tells you nothing until you know who was allowed to vote.
A market claim works the same way. Before anyone can call the market strong
or weak, they have to say which stocks they measured. That chosen list is
called a universe. Choosing it well is the first skill this course teaches.

## What this module teaches

Five terms that get used interchangeably: company, share, exchange, index,
and research universe. How India's thousands of listings narrow to the 500
stocks Marketworks studies. Why the course starts there, and what the list
of 500 cannot tell you.

# Part 1 — Five terms, five different things

"I bought Tata Motors on the NSE, it's in the Nifty" names four different
things in one breath. The rest of the course depends on telling them apart.

## The company

A company is the business itself: factories, employees, brands, software,
debts, cash coming in and going out. It exists whether or not anyone trades
it.

## The share and its cousins

One share is a small slice of ownership in that business, a claim on part of
what it earns and owns. The market's word for any tradable claim is a
security. An ordinary share is one kind. Exchanges also trade ETFs (funds
you buy like a stock), REITs (slices of rental-property income), InvITs
(slices of infrastructure income), bonds, and more. Your broker app shows
all of them mixed together, which matters in a moment.

## The exchange

An exchange is the regulated marketplace where securities trade. It is the
venue, not the merchandise. India has more than one. NSE and BSE are the two
big ones, and most large companies are listed on both. Add "companies on
NSE" to "companies on BSE" and you count Reliance twice; the two lists
overlap.

SEBI, the market regulator, maintains the official list of recognized
exchanges: [SEBI — details of stock exchanges](https://www.sebi.gov.in/stock-exchanges.html).

## The index

An index is a measuring stick: a basket of stocks picked by written rules,
tracked as one number so people can discuss how the basket did.

An index is picked the way a cricket team is picked. There are written
selection rules and scheduled reviews, and players get added and dropped.
Selection means a player met the criteria on selection day. It is not a
lifetime guarantee, and it says nothing about form next season.

## The research universe

A research universe is the list of stocks you decided to ask your question
about. Different questions need different lists. "How are India's biggest
companies doing?" works with the Nifty 50. "Are mid-sized and smaller
companies rising too?" needs a wider list. "Which tiny companies are
gaining?" needs a different list again, plus a harder conversation about
risk. No universe is correct in general; a universe fits your question or it
doesn't.

"Whatever my broker app shows" is not a universe. That is a wedding guest
list copied from your entire contacts app: complete, and useless for
planning.

# Part 2 — From thousands of listings to 500

The whole journey on one page:

```text
        v

        |  and out of? not suspended?
        v
NIFTY 500  <- the official index
        |  100 large companies
        |  150 mid-sized companies
        |  250 smaller companies
        v

        the Nifty 500 list as it stood on a specific date,
        plus a record of which stocks we have usable data for
```

## The starting pool

NSE's year-end summary reported 2,898 companies listed on NSE as of
31 December 2025, with 2,358 on the main board. The count excludes mutual
funds, ETFs, and bonds. The date matters: companies list, delist, and merge
all year, so a count without a date goes stale without warning you.

Source: [NSE Annual Highlights — Calendar Year 2025](https://nsearchives.nseindia.com/web/pressrelease/2026-01/PR_cc_01012026_20260101194417.pdf).

## The case against studying all 2,900

"Everything" sounds thorough. In practice it mixes companies that behave
nothing alike as data. Some stocks trade crores of rupees a day; others
barely trade at all. A stock that rarely trades can jump 15% on a handful of
shares, and the move is noise rather than information. A Rs 5 share can look
cheap, but the price of one share says nothing about what the whole company
is worth.

Narrowing the list is not a verdict on the excluded companies. It builds a
first laboratory of stocks similar enough that comparing them means
something.

# Part 3 — The Nifty 500

The official index this course starts from is the Nifty 500.

One naming note. Inside Marketworks you will sometimes see the shorthand
"NSE 500". This course keeps the names separate: Nifty 500 means the
official index, and "Marketworks Nifty 500-derived universe" means our dated
copy of its member list with our data attached. NSE is an exchange. The
Nifty 500 is an index.

## The selection rules in plain words

The rules are written by NSE Indices, the cricket selectors from Part 1. To
qualify, a stock must:

- be an ordinary share of an Indian company trading on NSE (no bonds,
  warrants, or suspended shares);
- have traded on at least 90% of days over the past six months;
- have enough shares available for the public to buy and sell (this public
  portion is called the free float; promoters' locked-in holdings do not
  count);
- be cheap to trade in and out of, meaning the price moves against you by no
  more than about 1% when you trade (this cost is called the impact cost);
- have some listing history, and rank in the top 800 on both trading
  activity and total company value.

From everything that qualifies, the index takes the 500 biggest companies by
six-month average value. Company value here means market capitalization:
share price times number of shares, roughly the cost of buying the whole
company.

The rules change from time to time. The authoritative version lives with NSE
Indices: [Nifty equity-index methodology, March 2026](https://www.niftyindices.com/Methodology/Method_NIFTY_Equity_Indices.pdf).

## Inside the 500

NSE Indices describes listed companies as a ladder by size. The biggest 100
are large cap, the next 150 mid cap, the 250 after that small cap, and
everything beyond microcap and smaller. ("Cap" is short for market
capitalization, company size.) The Nifty 500 is the top three rungs
together: you see the giants, the middle, and the smaller players, without
the thinnest end of the market.

Source: [Nifty 500 white paper, October 2025](https://www.niftyindices.com/docs/default-source/indices/nifty-500/nifty-500-whitepaper_2025.pdf?sfvrsn=7b2c6635_4).

## Unequal votes

"The Nifty 500 rose 1%" is one number built from 500 stocks, and the votes
are unequal. Each company counts in proportion to the market value of its
publicly tradable shares. A top bank moves the index far more than the 400th
company on the list. The official term is free-float market-cap weighted;
the plain version is that big companies' moves count for more. Part 5 turns
on this fact.

## A dated list

The Nifty 500 is refreshed twice a year, with changes taking effect on the
last working day of March and September. Stocks join and stocks drop out.

Source: [Nifty Indices reconstitution calendar](https://niftyindices.com/resources/index-rebalancing-schedule).

"The Nifty 500" is a series of dated lists, so an analysis has to say which
date's list it used. Marketworks stamps that date on its data everywhere.

# Part 4 — The case for starting here

Coverage: as of 30 March 2026, NSE Indices reported the Nifty 500 covered
about 92% of the market value of all NSE-listed stocks (measured on free
float) and about 84% of everything traded over the prior six months. The
percentages drift, which is why they carry a date, but the picture is
stable: most of the listed market's value and trading sits inside this list.
Source: [Nifty 500 official index page](https://www.niftyindices.com/indices/equity/broad-based-indices/nifty-500).

Range: the Nifty 50 describes the giants. It cannot say whether hundreds of
mid and small companies joined a rally or sat it out, and that difference is
what this course teaches you to see.

Pre-filtering: every member passed the trading-regularity, free-float, and
cost-to-trade checks, which clears the worst data problems before we start.
Filtered is not the same as recommended; the checks test tradability, not
quality.

Enough stocks to compare: Marketworks keeps asking questions like "how many
stocks are above their long-term trend?" Those questions need a crowd, and
500 is a crowd you can still understand.

A shared measuring stick: if every learner picked their own stock list, no
two readings could be compared. With a shared, dated universe, your number
and mine can disagree for interesting reasons instead of because we counted
different stocks.

# Part 5 — The index can rise while most stocks fall

A class average can rise because two toppers scored 98 while most of the
class slipped. "The average rose" and "most students did worse" are both
true; they answer different questions. An index is that average, and from
Part 3 you know the biggest companies are toppers with extra weight.

Take a toy index of ten stocks in which the two biggest carry half the total
weight between them. One day those two rise and the other eight fall. The
weighted index finishes up about +0.3% while eight of ten stocks fell. You
will run these numbers yourself in the worksheet.

Two honest headlines describe that day. "The index rose." "Only 20% of
stocks rose." The first describes the weighted basket. The second counts how
many stocks joined the move; the market's word for that head-count is
breadth, and in a breadth count every stock gets one vote regardless of
size.

Marketworks shows both on purpose. The index alone can hide a rally carried
by three giants. Breadth alone hides where the money is. Read them together.

# Part 6 — What the list cannot tell you

Every universe leaves things out, and naming the blind spots is what
separates a measurement from a guess. Starting with the Nifty 500 does not
mean:

- these are 500 good companies (the filters test tradability, not quality or
  price);
- they are all equally easy to trade (a small-cap member trades far thinner
  than a large bank);
- India's smallest companies are represented (the microcap tail sits
  outside);
- membership is a recommendation (it never is);
- today's list was always the list (members change twice a year).

## Judging the past with today's list

The last point produces one of the most common mistakes in market analysis.
Today's Nifty 500 is a photograph of today. Study "how these 500 stocks
performed over the last ten years" and you are studying only the companies
that survived and succeeded enough to be on today's list. The ones that
shrank, failed, or delisted along the way are missing from your record.

The name for this mistake is survivorship bias: judging a journey by
interviewing only the people who finished it. Module 3 treats it properly.
Until then, one sentence covers it: a universe list is data, and data has a
date.

## Members and measured are different counts

A metric like "% of stocks above their 200-day average" needs 200 days of
price history per stock. A company that listed recently does not have that
yet. On a given day Marketworks may compute the metric on 480 of the 500,
and the display should say so.

The official side has its own wrinkle: around a merger or an index
maintenance event, the official dated member file can briefly hold a few
more or fewer than 500 securities. When that happens we report the official
count as published. We do not round it to 500, and we do not invent a
reason.

A statistic you can trust answers four things: counted how many, out of
which list, as of which date, and with what left out.

# The habit this module leaves you with

Whenever anyone, including Marketworks and including this course, shows you
a market statistic, run this checklist:

```text
Which stocks were measured, exactly?
Why is that the right list for this question?
How did stocks get onto the list?
Does every stock count equally, or do big ones count more?
Which date's list is this?
How many stocks were actually counted?
What's outside the list that I should remember exists?
```

A statistic that cannot answer these is not wrong. It is unready.

# Closing

The Nifty 500 avoids two bad extremes. The 50-stock headline view misses
most of the market. The everything-in-the-app view drowns you in noise. In
between sits a wide, rules-based, dated list, built for learning how India's
large, mid, and small companies move together and apart.

Module 2 asks the first working question: when the index rises, is the whole
market rising, or is the index hiding what most stocks are doing?

How many stocks are in the Indian market?

Five words that sound the same but aren’t: company, share, exchange, index, and your research universe.

If you said 50, that’s a reasonable guess — the Nifty 50 is the number in the news every evening. If you said 500, also reasonable: Marketworks talks about 500 stocks all the time. And if you’ve ever typed a random letter into your broker app and scrolled, “thousands, surely” feels right too.

Here’s the strange part: every one of those answers is correct about something. Think of it like an election. If someone says “the people have voted yes,” your first question should be: which people? The result means nothing until you know who was on the voter list. Markets work the same way. Before anyone can call the market strong, weak, or expensive, they have to say which stocks they measured. That chosen list has a name: your universe.

Part 01 · Five words

Five words that sound the same but aren’t

“I bought Tata Motors on the NSE, it’s in the Nifty” — one breath, four different things. Let’s pull them apart, because the rest of the course leans on these distinctions.

Company
The actual business: the factories, the employees, the brands, the debts, the cash coming in and going out. It exists whether or not anyone trades it.
Security
When you buy a share, you’re buying a small slice of ownership in a business. The market’s general word for any tradable claim like this is a security. An ordinary share is one kind of security. Exchanges also trade others: funds you can buy and sell like a single stock (ETFs), securities that hand you a slice of rental-property income (REITs), securities that hand you a slice of infrastructure income like roads and power lines (InvITs), and bonds — and your broker app shows all of them mixed together.
Exchange
The regulated marketplace where securities trade — the venue, not the merchandise. NSE and BSE are the two big ones in India, and most large companies are listed on both.
Index
A measuring stick, not a place or a product. It’s a basket of stocks picked by written rules, tracked as one number — picked like a cricket team: rules, a scheduled review, players dropped and added.
Research universe
The list of stocks you’ve decided to ask your question about. Different questions need different lists — there’s no single correct universe, only one that fits your question or doesn’t.
The broker-search trap

“Whatever my broker app shows me” is not a universe. That’s planning a wedding by inviting your entire phone contacts list — technically complete, useless for the actual decision.

Part 02 · The funnel

From thousands of listings to a workable 500

Here’s the whole journey on one page. We narrow the market one deliberate step at a time — and each step changes what the list can answer.

One trap right at the top: most large companies are listed on both NSE and BSE. So you can’t count “companies on NSE” plus “companies on BSE” and call the total “Indian companies” — you’d be counting Reliance twice. The two lists overlap heavily.

And why not just study everything? “Everything” sounds thorough, but it mixes companies that are nothing alike as data. Some stocks trade crores of rupees every day; others barely trade at all. A stock that hardly trades can show a dramatic 15% jump on a day when only a handful of shares changed hands — the move looks like information, but it’s mostly noise. And a beginner can look at a ₹5 share and think “cheap!”, when the price of one share tells you nothing about what the whole company is worth.

Narrowing the list isn’t a judgment that the excluded companies are bad. It’s about building a clean first laboratory — a set of stocks similar enough that comparing them actually means something.

The list follows the question

“How are the giants doing?”, “Is the whole market joining the rally?”, and “Which tiny companies are quietly gaining?” are three different questions. Each needs its own list — don’t let them share one by accident.

Part 03 · The index

Meet the Nifty 500

One naming note first. Inside Marketworks you’ll sometimes see the shorthand “NSE 500.” In this course we’ll be precise: the official index is called the Nifty 500.

Nifty 500 means the official index. Marketworks Nifty 500-derived universe means our dated copy of its member list, with our data attached. Keeping those names separate stops you from ever confusing an exchange (NSE) with an index (Nifty 500).

Remember the cricket-team idea — a team picked by written rules, reviewed on a schedule? Here are the actual selection rules, as written by NSE Indices, in everyday language. To be eligible, a stock must:

  • Be an ordinary share of an Indian company trading on NSE — not a bond, a suspended share, or another non-share instrument
  • Not be one of the excluded security types the rules spell out
  • Have enough shares genuinely available for the public to buy and sell — the publicly tradable portion is called the free float
  • Have actually traded on at least 90% of days over the past six months — no ghost stocks
  • Be cheap to get in and out of: trading shouldn’t move the price against you by more than about 1% (the market calls this slippage the impact cost)
  • Have some listing history if it joined the market recently
  • Rank among the top 800 stocks on both trading activity and total company value
  • From everything that qualifies: the 500 biggest companies by average total value over six months make the index

That’s our plain-language version. The rules do change; the authoritative version is the official Nifty equity-index methodology, March 2026.

What the 500 contains

One word before the ladder: “cap” is just short for market capitalization — a company’s size, roughly what it would cost to buy the whole company (share price × number of shares).

100Large capThe biggest companies
150Mid capThe next 150 by size
250Small capThe 250 after that
Outside the starting listMicrocap and everything smaller →

NSE Indices describes India’s listed companies as a ladder by size. The Nifty 500 is the top three rungs together: you see the giants and the middle and the smaller players, without yet wading into the thinnest, hardest-to-trade end of the market.

When the news says “the Nifty 500 rose 1%,” how do 500 stocks become one number? Not by equal votes. Each company counts in proportion to the market value of its publicly tradable shares — its free float. The official term is free-float market-cap weighted; the plain version is: big companies’ moves count for more. Hold onto that — it becomes the key to Part 5.

The list is not permanent. It’s refreshed twice a year — under the current schedule, changes take effect on the last working day of March and September — and events like mergers, suspensions, and delistings can trigger changes in between. So “the Nifty 500” is really a series of dated lists. Membership is data, and it has a date.

Part 04 · Why here

Why Marketworks begins here

Out of every possible universe, why this one? Five reasons — none of which is “these are the best stocks.”

Share of everything traded on NSE over the prior six months84.07%

Official Nifty 500 page · as of 30 March 2026 · these percentages drift over time — hence the date

01It covers most of the money

This list captures the overwhelming bulk of where India’s listed money sits and trades — while keeping the unmeasured tail honestly visible.

02All three sizes

The Nifty 50 can tell you about giants. It can’t tell you whether hundreds of mid and small companies are joining a rally or sitting it out — exactly what this course teaches you to see.

03Pre-filtered for tradability

Every member already passed the trading-regularity, free-float, and cost-to-trade checks. That clears the noisiest data problems — but filtered is not the same as recommended.

04A real crowd

Questions like “how many stocks are above their long-term trend?” need a crowd. Five hundred is a real crowd that’s still small enough to understand.

05Everyone measures the same thing

A shared, dated list means your number and my number can disagree for interesting reasons — not because we counted different stocks.

What starting here does not mean

  • a sign these are 500 good companies — the filters check tradability, not quality or price
  • a promise they’re all equally easy to trade — a small-cap member is far thinner than a large bank
  • the whole market — the microcap tail sits entirely outside
  • a permanent list — members come and go every six months, so old studies on today’s list quietly cheat
  • a recommendation to buy, hold, or sell anything — index membership never is
Part 05 · The payoff

The index went up. Did most stocks?

Think of a school exam. The class average can rise because two toppers scored 98 while most of the class slipped. Indexes work exactly like that average — and the “toppers” carry extra weight.

Two honest headlines for the same day: “the index rose” and “eight of ten stocks fell.” Both are true. They answer different questions.

The index tells you what happened to the weighted basket. The head-count tells you how many stocks actually joined the move — the market’s word for this is breadth, and it gives every stock one equal vote, big or small. Marketworks shows you both views on purpose: the index without breadth can hide a rally carried by three giants; breadth without the index can hide where the actual money is. Neither is “the real market.” Read them together.

Part 06 · Honest counting

“500 members” doesn’t mean “500 measured”

Every universe leaves things out. Naming your blind spots isn’t pessimism — it’s the difference between a measurement and a guess.

First, the time-travel trap. Today’s Nifty 500 list is a photograph of today. Take that list and study “how these 500 performed over the last ten years” and you’ve quietly cheated: you’re interviewing only the companies that survived and succeeded enough to be in the index today. The ones that shrank, failed, or got delisted have vanished from your study. This is called survivorship bias — judging a journey by interviewing only the people who finished it. Module 3 tackles it properly.

Second, an honest wrinkle. A metric like “% of stocks above their 200-day average” — that average being each stock’s average closing price over its last 200 trading days, a slow-moving line that shows its long-term trend — needs 200 days of price history per stock, and a recent joiner doesn’t have that yet. So on a given day, Marketworks might compute a metric on, say, 480 of the 500, and the display should tell you so. The reverse happens too: around a merger or an index maintenance event, even the official dated member file can briefly hold slightly more or fewer than 500 securities. When that occurs, we report the official count as published — we never quietly round it to 500 or invent a reason.

The habit

Any statistic you trust should answer: counted how many, out of what list, as of when, and what was left out. A percentage without those answers is a number wearing a costume.

01
What is a research universe?

A universe is the chosen, stated list a question is asked about. A broker search mixes everything; an exchange is a venue; your holdings are a portfolio.

02
Why can’t you add NSE and BSE company counts to get “Indian companies”?

The two lists overlap heavily. Add them and you’re counting Reliance twice.

03
Which of these best describes the official Nifty 500?

Written rules decide who qualifies, who gets picked, how they’re weighted, and when the list is reviewed. It’s not every listing, not a price ranking, and not ours to choose.

04
Which company sizes make up the Nifty 500, as this course describes it?

The 500 is the top three rungs of the size ladder: the biggest 100, the next 150, and the 250 after that.

05
Why is the Nifty 500 a useful place for Marketworks to start?

It’s a wide, rules-based, shared laboratory. It is not a quality certificate, and it leaves the smallest companies out.

06
The Nifty 500 is “free-float market-cap weighted” — the plain version being: big companies’ moves count for more. What does that mean in practice?

Weight follows the market value of the shares the public can actually trade. The price of one share, on its own, tells you nothing about a company’s size.

07 · Required
A ten-stock weighted index rose, but only two of the ten stocks went up. What’s the right way to read that?

“The index rose” and “only 2 of 10 stocks rose” can both be true. The weighted basket and the one-vote-each head-count (breadth) answer different questions.

08
Why must a universe always carry a date?

Companies list, delist, join, and leave — a count without a date goes stale silently. But a date alone doesn’t fix the time-travel trap; studying history honestly needs the list as it stood back then.

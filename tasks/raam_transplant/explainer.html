Research note · in plain English

Are our 24 momentum stocks secretly one big bet?

We borrowed one idea from a prize-winning paper — measuring how “crowded” a portfolio is — and tested whether it makes our Core Momentum strategy safer. Here’s the whole story, no jargon.

The idea, in one picture

A momentum portfolio buys “what’s going up.” The hidden danger: the 24 things going up are often the same thing in disguise.

What it looks like

24 different tickers. Looks nicely spread out and diversified.

What it really is

Really 3 theme bets (say defence, PSU, small-caps). If one theme cracks, a third of the book falls together.

We call this crowding. It’s invisible on a normal holdings list — you only see it if you measure how the stocks actually move together.

What we set out to do

You asked me to review a prize-winning trading paper (the “Ranked Asset Allocation Model”) and see if anything in it could sharpen our strategies. Most of its ingredients we already use in stronger forms. But one idea was genuinely new to us: scoring investments on how crowded / correlated they are, so you can lean away from hidden concentration.

The goal: take our Core Momentum strategy (the one that holds the top 24 NSE 500 stocks and reshuffles weekly) and test a crowding-aware version — does nudging out the 2–3 most crowded names each week make it safer without costing returns?

How we test — the honest rules

Set the bar firstWe write down the pass/fail targets before running anything, so we can’t move the goalposts to make a result look good.
Fresh examWe tune on old data, then test on newer years the strategy has never seen — like revising past papers but sitting a brand-new one.
Real costsEvery number is after trading costs. If an “improvement” is just churn that eats itself, it won’t show up as a gain.
Keep the “no”sIdeas that fail get recorded and dropped — a fast, cheap “no” is a good outcome, not a wasted week.

It does, mildly: when the book is more crowded, the next month’s worst dip tends to be a bit deeper — without higher returns to justify it. We also tested a second idea (a “market-breadth” dial) and it failed, so we dropped it. That’s the method working.

The headline result

Core Momentum (today’s strategy) vs. the crowding-aware version, across three test periods it never saw during tuning. Higher return is better; a smaller worst-drop is safer.

The plain reading: the crowding-aware version earned a little more in every period and had a smaller worst-drop in two of the three — all after costs. We expected, at best, “same return, safer.” We got “slightly better return and safer.”

Honest caveats: the gain is modest, and in the choppy 2017–19 stretch it was marginally worse on drops (there’s little crowding to fix when nothing’s trending). This is a promising, well-tested candidate — not a decision to change the live product yet.

Where this leaves us

One idea from the paper survived contact with our data — crowding — and it produced a modest, well-tested improvement to Core Momentum plus a genuinely useful risk gauge. A second idea was tested and honestly discarded. That’s a healthy hit rate for a research probe.
